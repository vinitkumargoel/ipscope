What is an IP address?

An IP address is the number the internet uses to route traffic to you. Every device that talks to the network needs one, the same way a letter needs a street address. When you load this page, your request arrives carrying a public IP address, and that is the address shown above.

Most people have two at once. An IPv4 address looks like 203.0.113.45 — four numbers, 32 bits in total, which caps the whole system at roughly 4.3 billion addresses. An IPv6 address looks like 2001:db8::42 — 128 bits, which is a supply so large it will not run out. IPv4 ran short years ago, so networks now run both side by side and prefer IPv6 where it is available. If the panel above shows both, your connection is dual-stack.

Public versus private addresses

The address your router shows you in its settings is usually a private one — something in 192.168.0.0/16, 10.0.0.0/8, or 172.16.0.0/12. Those ranges are reserved for use inside a home or office network and are not routable across the internet. Your router translates between them and the single public address your ISP assigned, a process called NAT. This page can only ever see the public address. That is the one websites, game servers, and mail hosts actually see too.

What your IP address reveals about you

Less than people fear, and more than is comfortable. From a public IP address, a site can generally determine your country with high confidence, your region or city with moderate confidence, and the name of your internet provider. It can also see the ASN — the network operator's identifier in the global routing table — and whether the address belongs to a residential ISP, a mobile carrier, or a datacentre.

What it does not reveal is your name, your street address, your device, or your identity. An IP address is not a home address. Only your ISP holds the record connecting an address to a subscriber, and in most jurisdictions releasing it requires legal process.

Why the city is often wrong

Geolocation works by looking your address up in a database that maps ranges to places. Those mappings come from registry records, ISP-published data, and network measurement — not from your device. So the result reflects where your provider registered or routes the address, which is frequently the nearest large interconnection point rather than your town. Mobile networks are the worst offenders, because carriers route large subscriber pools through a handful of gateways. Treat city-level results as approximate and expect them to be tens of kilometres out. We publish a longer explanation in why your IP location shows the wrong city.

VPNs, proxies, and timezone mismatches

If you are connected through a VPN, everything above describes the VPN server, not you. That is the point of a VPN. A common way sites detect one anyway is to compare the timezone your browser reports against the timezone expected for your IP address — a VPN moves the address but not your operating-system clock, so the two disagree. You can test that directly with the timezone check, or test whether an address looks like a VPN, proxy, or hosting range with the VPN and proxy check.

How IPScope resolves lookups

Geolocation runs entirely on this server against offline MaxMind-compatible MMDB databases — DB-IP and GeoLite2, merged so that a gap in one is filled by the other. There is no third-party geolocation API in the request path, no API key, and no per-lookup fee. Registry details such as the network range and abuse contact come from public RDAP services, and map tiles come from OpenStreetMap. Everything else is local.

That design has an honest trade-off: offline databases are a snapshot, so a range reassigned last week may still resolve to its former owner. It also means we cannot see anything about you that your request did not already carry.
