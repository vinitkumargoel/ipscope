PTR resolution runs on this server with a 2.5 second timeout and results are cached for one hour. A timeout is reported as “no record”, because from the outside the two look the same.

What a reverse DNS lookup actually does

Ordinary DNS turns a name into an address. Reverse DNS goes the other way: it asks whether whoever controls an IP address has published a name for it. There is no special protocol involved — it is a normal DNS query for a PTR record inside a reserved zone. An IPv4 address is written backwards label by label under in-addr.arpa, so a lookup of 203.0.113.45 is really a query for 45.113.0.203.in-addr.arpa. IPv6 works the same way but per hex digit under ip6.arpa, which is why an IPv6 reverse name has 32 labels in it.

The important detail is who gets to answer. A forward record is controlled by whoever owns the domain. A PTR record is controlled by whoever holds the address block — normally the ISP, hosting company, or corporate network, not the person sitting behind the address. Regional registries delegate the reverse zone for a block down to the operator, and the operator decides whether to publish anything at all. Almost every quirk on this page follows from that one arrangement.

When there is no PTR record

An empty answer is a normal answer. Large numbers of residential, mobile, and CGNAT addresses have no PTR record, and nothing breaks as a result. Browsing, streaming, gaming, and video calls do not consult reverse DNS. Operators skip it because they have millions of addresses and no obligation to name them, or they publish records only for static assignments and business customers. So a blank result is not evidence that an address is unused, suspicious, or misconfigured — for most purposes it is simply the absence of information.

There is one place where absence is a genuine signal, and that is email. Many receiving mail servers treat a sending address with no PTR record as a strong spam indicator, and some reject the connection outright before the message body is ever sent. If you are debugging outbound mail from your own server and this lookup comes back empty, you have found something worth fixing with your hosting provider. If you are looking up somebody else's residential address, an empty result tells you close to nothing.

How to read a hostname you do get

Where a PTR record does exist, it was almost certainly generated by the operator's provisioning system from a template rather than typed by a person. That is good news for interpretation, because templates leak structure. Three kinds of information tend to end up in the name.

The operator

The registered domain at the end of the hostname is the most reliable part of it. A name ending in an ISP's own domain tells you which organisation was handed the block, and it usually agrees with the ASN and registry data shown next to the result. When they disagree — the PTR says one company and the registry says another — the block has probably changed hands or been sublet, and the registry data is the fresher of the two. Cross-check with the abuse contact lookup, which reads directly from RDAP rather than from DNS.

The access technology

Middle labels often describe how the address is delivered. These conventions are informal, vary between operators, and are not standardised anywhere, but the same handful of abbreviations recurs constantly.

  • dsl, adsl, vdsl, dslam — a copper DSL line, often a consumer connection.
  • cable, cbl, docsis, hfc — a cable modem on a hybrid fibre-coax plant.
  • fibre, fiber, ftth, gpon — fibre to the premises.
  • dyn, dynamic, pool, dhcp — a dynamically assigned address that may move between customers.
  • static, fixed — a fixed assignment, more likely to be a business line or a server.
  • mobile, lte, gprs, umts, wireless — a cellular or fixed-wireless network.
  • vps, srv, host, cloud, colo, dedi — hosting or datacentre infrastructure.
  • cust, customer, res, bb, broadband — a customer access line rather than the operator's own equipment.
  • mail, mx, smtp, ns, dns — a named service host, configured deliberately.
  • gw, gateway, core, edge, bdr — router or aggregation infrastructure inside the network.

A hostname that contains the address itself, such as 203-0-113-45.example.net or host-203-0-113-45.example.net, was generated automatically for the whole block. It tells you the operator's naming scheme and nothing about that particular address.

The geographic hint

Operators frequently embed a city name, an exchange or point-of-presence code, or an airport-style three letter code. When the hint is present and matches the geolocation database, you have two weakly independent sources agreeing, which is more than you had before. When it disagrees, you cannot tell which one is wrong without more evidence.

Treat geographic hints as the weakest part of the name. Reverse zones are populated once and rarely revisited. Blocks get moved between cities, providers merge and keep the acquired company's naming template, and traffic from a customer in one city can be aggregated through a router named after another. A hostname is evidence about where an address was provisioned at some unknown point in the past, not proof of where the user is now. IP geolocation itself is only accurate to roughly city level in the best case, so agreement between a stale hostname and an approximate database is not confirmation of anything precise.

Forward-confirmed reverse DNS

A PTR record on its own is an unverified claim. The address holder can point it at almost any name they like, including a name belonging to someone else, because nothing in the reverse zone proves that the named domain agrees. Forward-confirmed reverse DNS (often abbreviated FCrDNS) closes that gap with a second step: resolve the PTR name back through normal forward DNS and check that the resulting A or AAAA records include the address you started from. If they do, two separately controlled zones agree, and the name is much harder to fake.

This is why mail systems, some SSH and FTP daemons, and various abuse-scoring pipelines insist on forward confirmation rather than a bare PTR record. It is also why a hostname alone should never be used as an authorisation or allow-list mechanism. This page performs only the PTR half of the check, because the forward half would need a resolver the browser does not expose; run it yourself with dig +short <hostname> or host <hostname> and compare the answer to the original address.

What else PTR records are for

Beyond mail reputation, reverse DNS mostly exists to make operational output readable. Traceroute and mtr print the PTR name of each hop, which is how you can see a path leaving a city, crossing a transit provider, and entering a destination network without looking up a single ASN by hand. Web and firewall logs are far easier to triage when the entries carry names instead of bare numbers, and many analytics and intrusion-detection tools resolve addresses for exactly that reason. Abuse handling benefits too: a hostname often points straight at the responsible operator, which is the first thing you need before filing a report.

The cost is that reverse lookups add latency and can be abused as a signal channel, so a lot of high-volume software has moved to resolving names after the fact rather than in the request path. If you need names for a batch of addresses, resolve them offline rather than inline.

Limits of this particular tool

  • Only the first PTR name is shown. An address is allowed to have several, and multiple PTR records are unusual but legal.
  • Resolution happens on this server using its configured resolver. A different resolver, or a lookup from inside the operator's own network, can legitimately return a different answer.
  • Results are cached for one hour, and your recursive resolver caches independently according to the zone's TTL. A record you have just changed may take a while to appear.
  • The 2.5 second timeout means a slow or broken reverse zone is indistinguishable from an unconfigured one. Both display as no record.
  • Private, loopback, and other reserved ranges have no meaningful public reverse DNS, so nothing is resolved for them.
  • The interpretation of tokens in a hostname is pattern matching against common conventions. It is a hint generator, not an authority.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my IP have no hostname?

Because your ISP never published one. Reverse DNS for your address is controlled by the operator that holds the block, not by you, and most consumer broadband and mobile providers leave individual addresses unnamed. It is normal and it does not affect your connection. If you need a PTR record for a mail server, you have to ask the provider that assigned the address to set it.

Can I set my own PTR record?

Only if you control the address block, or your provider offers reverse DNS delegation. On a VPS or dedicated server this is usually a field in the control panel. On residential broadband it is normally not offered at all. Adding an A record that points a name at your address does not create a PTR record — the two directions are separate zones with separate owners.

Does the hostname prove where the IP is located?

No. A city or airport code inside a hostname reflects how the block was provisioned at some point in the past, and reverse zones are rarely updated after that. Treat it as one weak signal to compare against the geolocation and registry data, never as proof. See the general FAQ for how approximate IP geolocation is in the first place.

Is reverse DNS the same as a WHOIS or RDAP lookup?

No. Reverse DNS reads a PTR record from the DNS, which the address holder edits at will. RDAP and WHOIS read the registry's record of who was allocated the block, including the abuse mailbox. Registry data is more authoritative for ownership questions; the abuse contact lookup uses it.

Why do two tools show different hostnames for the same IP?

Caching and resolver differences. Each tool caches for its own interval, each recursive resolver honours the zone's TTL separately, and an address with more than one PTR record can legitimately return them in a different order. If a record changed recently, expect disagreement for a while.
